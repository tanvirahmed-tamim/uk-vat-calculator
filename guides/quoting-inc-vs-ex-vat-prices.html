How to Quote Prices to VAT-Registered vs. Non-VAT Customers (Inc. vs. Ex. VAT)

By the VAT in UK team · Last reviewed 21 November 2026 · 8 min read

Part of VAT calculation

The same service can be quoted at two very different prices depending on who is buying. This guide explains when to quote net, when to quote gross, and how to keep a quotation clear enough that nobody argues about the VAT later.

In short

Consumers must see the price they actually pay, including VAT. VAT-registered businesses can be quoted net prices, provided the VAT rate and amount are stated clearly. At 20% the VAT is one sixth of the gross price, so a £1,200 gross quote is £1,000 net plus £200 VAT.

Who this is for: UK businesses preparing quotes for mixed B2B and B2C customers, and sole traders unsure whether their price should include VAT.

Should a quote include VAT?

A quote to a consumer must show the price they will actually pay, including VAT. A quote to a VAT-registered business can show prices excluding VAT, provided the VAT position is stated clearly on the document.

The rule behind that comes from the CAP Code on misleading advertising, which says quoted prices must include non-optional taxes that apply to all or most buyers. A VAT-exclusive price is allowed only where everyone the price is addressed to pays no VAT or can recover it, and then the VAT rate or amount must be shown prominently.

In practice, that means two versions of the same quote may both be correct. The mistake is sending the business version to a private customer.

Who can reclaim the VAT?

A VAT-registered business can usually reclaim input VAT on business purchases, while a consumer cannot reclaim anything. The gross price is therefore the real cost for a private customer and a recoverable cost for a registered business.

How to quote different customer types
Customer Can reclaim VAT? Quote basis Document
VAT-registered business Yes, on business purchases Net, with VAT shown separately VAT invoice
Private consumer No Gross, VAT-inclusive VAT invoice or receipt
Business below the VAT threshold No Gross, VAT-inclusive VAT invoice
VAT-exempt business No, exempt sales carry no input tax recovery Gross, VAT-inclusive VAT invoice

Ask for a VAT number before you send a net quote to a business. It takes a moment, and it prevents a dispute about whether VAT was ever part of the agreed price. A business that is not registered still pays the VAT you charge.

Net and gross: the arithmetic

The net price is the amount before VAT, the gross price is the amount after VAT, and the difference is the VAT itself. At 20%, multiply the net figure by 1.20 to reach the gross, or divide the gross by 1.20 to return to the net.

The two display conventions are easy to mix up. VAT inclusive pricing puts the tax inside the headline figure, while VAT exclusive pricing shows it as a separate addition. Neither is wrong in the right context, but a quote that switches between them halfway down the page will confuse any customer.

To pull VAT out of a gross figure, multiply by the VAT fraction of one sixth. A £1,200 gross invoice contains £200 of VAT and £1,000 net, because 1,200 divided by 6 is 200.

  • £850 net at 20% is £170 VAT and £1,020 gross.
  • £1,020 gross at 20% is £170 VAT and £850 net.
  • £480 net at 5% is £24 VAT and £504 gross.
  • £64 net at 0% stays £64, with no VAT to add.

The VAT fraction calculator handles the division, and the main VAT calculator works in both directions. If you are building the gross figure from scratch, the guide to adding VAT sets out the multipliers.

How to lay out a quote

A clear quote shows the net total, the VAT rate and amount, and the gross total, with a sentence that says whether VAT is included. Consumers should see the gross figure as the headline number, with the VAT breakdown underneath.

Sample quote for four office chairs at £120 net each
Description Qty Unit net Net total VAT at 20% Gross total
Office chair, model A 4 £120.00 £480.00 £96.00 £576.00
Totals 4 £480.00 £96.00 £576.00

For a business customer, add a line such as "Prices exclude VAT. VAT at 20% will be added. Total including VAT: £576.00." For a consumer, lead with £576.00 and show the £96.00 VAT as the breakdown.

State how long the quote is valid, whether delivery is included and whether the price assumes a particular VAT rate. If you quote a fixed gross price to a consumer and the rate changes before the work is done, the agreed price usually stands unless your terms say otherwise.

When does a quote become a VAT invoice?

A quotation is not a VAT invoice and never becomes one, however detailed it is. The tax point arises when you issue a VAT invoice or make the supply, whichever happens first.

A valid VAT invoice must show your name, address and VAT registration number, a unique invoice number and the date, the customer's name and address, a description of what was supplied, the unit price excluding VAT, the rate and amount of VAT, and the totals excluding and including VAT.

Quote compared with VAT invoice
Point Quotation VAT invoice
Creates a tax point No Yes, on issue or supply
VAT number required Not required Required
Customer can reclaim No Yes, with a valid invoice
VAT breakdown Good practice Required

Some businesses send a proforma invoice before payment. A proforma is not a VAT invoice either, and it does not create a tax point. The rules on invoice content are covered in VAT invoices and MTD.

Quoting overseas customers

A quote to a customer outside the UK needs a different VAT line, because UK VAT may not apply at all. Goods exported from the UK are normally zero-rated once the evidence shows they left, and many services supplied to overseas businesses fall outside the scope of UK VAT.

Do not simply strip the VAT out and reuse the domestic wording. State the basis clearly: the goods are zero-rated for export, or the service is outside the scope of UK VAT, and the customer accounts for any tax due in their own country. An export quote without that wording invites the customer to assume the price includes their local tax.

Delivery terms matter to the commercial deal even when they do not change the VAT treatment. A quote on delivered duty paid terms carries a different risk from one where the customer collects. Whichever you choose, the quote should say who arranges freight, who pays import charges and which currency the figures use.

The place of supply guide and the imports and exports guide deal with the underlying rules.

Deposits, stage payments and estimates

A quote is not a demand for money, but the moment you take a deposit the VAT question starts. A deposit that is part payment for a supply creates a tax point when it is received, and VAT is due on that amount in the period it arrives.

A refundable deposit held as security is different. If it is not consideration for a supply and you may have to return it, no VAT arises when it is taken. The distinction is whether the money is payment for something or merely a guarantee.

Stage payments on a longer project follow the same rule. Each payment on account creates a tax point for the value received, so a quote that sets out stages should also say when VAT becomes due on each one. Leaving that out is how businesses end up with a VAT bill before the project has generated the cash to cover it.

An estimate is not a quote. An estimate says what the work might cost, and a customer who accepts it has not agreed a fixed price. If you want the protection of a fixed figure, send a quotation that states how long it stays valid. The discounts and deposits guide covers the VAT mechanics in more detail.

Discounts, prompt payment and VAT rate changes

A discount reduces the consideration, so it reduces the VAT. If you quote £1,000 less a 10% trade discount, VAT is charged on £900, not on £1,000.

Prompt payment discounts follow the amount actually paid, provided the discount was offered at the time of supply. If the customer pays early and takes the discount, VAT is accounted for on the discounted price. If they pay late and lose it, VAT is due on the full amount.

Rate changes are rarer but harder. The rate in force at the tax point applies, so work quoted at 20% and invoiced after a change is taxed at the new rate unless the supply itself happened earlier. A fixed gross price agreed with a consumer can leave you absorbing the difference, which is why long quotations should state the rate they assume.

Check how your template rounds as well. A quote built from rounded net prices can drift by a penny or two per line once VAT is added at 20%. The bulk calculator shows the reconciliation line by line.

Quotes that mix VAT rates

When a quote covers items at different rates, the customer needs to see each rate separately. A single total with one VAT figure hides which part attracted 20% and which part was zero-rated, and that is exactly the detail a business customer needs to reclaim input tax.

A builder quoting for a renovation is the classic case: materials may be standard-rated while certain work on a qualifying property is reduced-rated, and some services sit at zero. Guessing at a blended rate produces a figure that reconciles to nothing and is hard to defend in a review.

Show the net amount for each rate band, the VAT at that rate, and then the total. If a discount applies across the quote, apply it before VAT and show how it was allocated, because a discount applied only to the standard-rated lines gives a different answer.

The mixed invoice guide sets out the rounding rules. For a quick check, the bulk calculator lets you put each line at its own rate.

Where a quote offers optional extras, price each option with its own net and VAT figures. A customer who declines an option should see the total fall by the amount quoted, including the VAT on it.

Quoting when you are not VAT registered

A business that is not VAT registered must not charge VAT or show a VAT number. Its quoted price is the full price the customer pays, and there is no VAT to separate out.

Do not describe a price as "plus VAT" if you are not registered, even if you expect to register soon. Either the business is registered at the time of the supply or it is not. Adding VAT without a registration is not a timing point, it is an error.

Registering changes the numbers. A sole trader charging £100 can keep charging £100 and absorb the £16.67 of VAT, or charge £120 and risk losing price-sensitive customers. That decision is worth making before the registration date, not after the first invoice.

Common mistakes

Most pricing disputes come from a handful of avoidable habits.

  • Sending a net quote to a consumer, who then expects to pay the lower figure.
  • Writing "plus VAT" without a rate or amount, which the CAP Code does not allow.
  • Treating a quotation as an invoice for payment terms or a tax point.
  • Forgetting that building a gross price upwards needs a different calculation from removing VAT from a gross figure.
  • Quoting rounded gross prices that no longer reconcile to the net and VAT lines.

The bulk VAT calculator is useful when a quote has many lines and the rounding has to reconcile. The guide to removing VAT explains why subtracting 20% from a gross price gives the wrong net figure.

Questions

Should a quote include VAT for UK businesses and consumers?
Quotes to consumers must show the total price including VAT, while quotes to VAT-registered businesses may show prices excluding VAT if the VAT position is stated clearly.
How do VAT-registered businesses quote prices to B2B and B2C customers?
Businesses usually quote net prices to other VAT-registered businesses and gross prices to consumers. The same product can therefore carry two different headline prices.
Do non-VAT customers pay VAT on quotes and invoices?
Yes, if you are VAT registered. A customer who cannot reclaim VAT still pays it, so the gross price is their real cost.
What is the difference between VAT-inclusive and VAT-exclusive prices?
A VAT-exclusive price is the net amount before VAT, and a VAT-inclusive price is the gross amount after VAT. At 20%, the VAT element is one sixth of the gross.
How should VAT be displayed on a quotation?
Show the net total, the VAT rate and amount, and the gross total, with a clear statement of whether VAT is included. Consumers should see the gross figure as the headline price.
When does a quote become a VAT invoice?
A quotation never becomes a VAT invoice. The tax point arises when you issue a VAT invoice or make the supply, whichever happens first.
How do non-VAT registered businesses quote prices?
A business that is not VAT registered must not charge VAT or show a VAT number. Its quoted price is the full price the customer pays.
